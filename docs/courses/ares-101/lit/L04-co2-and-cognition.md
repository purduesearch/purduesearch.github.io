  This is a single-blinded randomised controlled trial of indoor air quality and cognition, run at
  the Fraunhofer Flight Test Facility in Holzkirchen, Germany, between December 2019 and January 2020.
  The facility is the front section of a former Airbus A310 inside a low-pressure vessel, so cabin
  altitude is a controllable variable rather than a confound. 398 healthy adults took part, 248 men
  and 150 women, mean age 42.7 years with a range of 18 to 69, recruited through an agency for
  background actors and stratified by age and sex to resemble the population of business air
  passengers. An a priori power calculation set the required sample at 391 for small-to-medium
  effects at alpha 5 percent and power 0.85. Sessions ran as simulated four-hour flights with a
  maximum of 40 participants seated one empty seat apart, at 23 degrees Celsius, 10 to 15 percent
  relative humidity, 74 dB(A) of noise, and an outdoor air supply rate of 9.4 litres per second per
  person. The study was approved by the LMU Munich medical ethics committee and every participant
  gave written informed consent. It was funded by Honeywell Aerospace UK, Airbus, Pall Aeropower,
  Embraer and Liebherr Aerospace, which a critical reader should register without treating it as
  disqualifying - the design, instruments and analysis are all reported in enough detail to be
  checked.

  Eleven exposure conditions crossed three CO2 levels, two atmospheric pressures and three VOC
  levels. CO2 was dosed as pure gas into the cabin supply air at 380, 2,480 and 4,440 litres per hour
  against a site outdoor background near 400 ppm, targeting 1,200, 2,750 and 4,200 ppm. Measured
  group averages came in a little under target - roughly 946 to 1,190 ppm in the low arms, 2,482 to
  2,733 in the middle, and 3,658 to 4,138 in the high. Pressure was either 940 hPa, the ground-level
  pressure at the site, or 755 hPa, corresponding to 8,000 feet and covering the full range permitted
  in aircraft cabins. The VOC mixture was twelve compounds chosen from those actually found in cabin
  air - ethanol, acetone, 1-propanol, 2-propanol, acetic acid, toluene, hexanal, acetonitrile,
  6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, nonanal, limonene and ethyl acetate - with IARC-listed carcinogens
  deliberately excluded, dosed to a nominal 266 micrograms per cubic metre at the medium level and
  798 at the high level, always on top of whatever background the cabin and its occupants produced.

  Two things about the exposure deserve to be carried forward, because they are what a careful reader
  takes away rather than what the abstract says. The first is the paper's own major limitation, which
  it states plainly - the VOC levels could not be held at their intended values. The group targeted
  at low VOC, low CO2 and 755 hPa came out with the highest measured TVOC of any condition, 2,103
  micrograms per cubic metre on average with ethanol alone at 1,514, on a day when nothing was dosed;
  the participants must have been the source, and the authors say frankly that they cannot explain
  it. The planned fractional factorial design had to be abandoned and every analysis was run as a
  one-factorial design with eleven factor levels, which means the study cannot separate independent
  main effects and interactions among its three factors. The second is arithmetic the paper does not
  do for you. Convert its exposures to partial pressure and no condition anywhere in the study
  exceeded about 2.6 mmHg - the highest is roughly 3,750 ppm at 940 hPa, which is 705 mmHg total, so
  about 2.64 mmHg - and NASA's operational cabin limit is 4 mmHg. The 755 hPa arms are lower still
  in partial-pressure terms than their ppm figures suggest, because reducing total pressure reduces
  the partial pressure delivered at a given mole fraction. Whatever this study bounds, it does not
  bound exposures at or above the limit the ISS actually operates against.

  The outcome measures are the study's strongest suit and are a deliberate argument against the
  earlier literature. Eight cognitive domains were assessed with established, published, standardised
  instruments - information processing speed by a German Trail-Making variant, concentration and
  sustained attention by the d2 test, figural working memory and logical reasoning by modules of the
  Intelligence Structure Test, executive control by a group Stroop, vigilance by an unannounced
  auditory stimulus presented while participants were occupied with something else, plus problem
  solving and proofreading. Repeated measures were taken twice, at roughly 55 to 85 minutes and 175
  to 205 minutes into an exposure held for about 165 minutes, and analyses controlled for age,
  chronotype and test familiarity, with education added for the two knowledge-dependent tests. The
  choice is pointed - the paper and the critics it cites object that the Strategic Management
  Simulation used by Satish and by Allen is a proprietary, closed-source instrument with unknown
  psychometric properties, which is precisely why this study does not use it, and which is also why
  its result is not a like-for-like refutation.

  The finding is a null. Differences reached significance in two of eight domains, concentration
  performance and figural working memory, and in neither case did the pattern order with CO2, VOC or
  pressure level - the best-performing group on concentration was neither the cleanest nor the
  dirtiest condition. Performance improved between the first and second test batteries in every
  group, consistent with normal learning rather than with accumulating impairment. Participants rated
  air quality as good to very good in all eleven conditions, with no significant difference between
  groups by either mean rating or distribution of judgements, and hardly perceived smell at all. The
  authors conclude that there is no evidence that short-term exposure to CO2 up to about 4,200 ppm,
  TVOCs up to about 2,100 micrograms per cubic metre, or cabin altitudes up to 8,000 feet had
  systematic negative effects on the cognitive abilities of healthy adults, and they place their
  result alongside a wider literature that is genuinely split - null or marginal results in the more
  rigorously designed studies, effects at 900 to 1,000 ppm in the SMS-based work, and no effects at
  all reported in submariners at 15,000 ppm and miners at 20,000 ppm.

  What the study licenses is narrower than either its own abstract or the headlines that will be
  written from it. It is a well-powered failure to detect an effect, which is real evidence against a
  large cognitive effect of bulk CO2 at these exposures in this population over this duration. It is
  not proof that no effect exists, it cannot separate its three factors, and it does not touch the
  vascular effects of CO2, which are not in dispute. For ARES the decisive limitation is not any of
  the ones the authors list. It is that this study dosed and measured bulk cabin air - CO2 injected
  into the supply duct, concentration read by a room analyser - with subjects seated an empty seat
  apart at 9.4 litres per second per person of ventilation and free to move. That is the bulk
  quantity, measured well. The quantity this course exists to measure is the localized concentration
  in front of a face held stationary for hours, which Dutta et al. model as roughly twice bulk cabin
  levels at its transient peaks in microgravity and which no instrument in this study went anywhere
  near. A learner who finishes this paper concluding that the CO2 bubble does not matter has drawn
  the one conclusion the design cannot support. The right conclusion is the uncomfortable one - the
  cognitive case for this project is weaker than the M4 deck implies, the physical case for measuring
  an unmeasured exposure is untouched by it, and those are different arguments that should stop being
  made as though they were one.

### Herbig, B., Mayer, F., Norrefeldt, V., & Wargocki, P. (2026). Do carbon dioxide, volatile organic compounds and atmospheric pressure affect the cognitive performance of occupants in indoor environments? Results of a large-scale experiment with simulated flights. *International Journal of Hygiene and Environmental Health*, 275, 114809.

The assigned reading, open access under CC BY. Read it in this order: the Abstract, then §2.2
(Exposure) with Tables 1 and 2, then §2.4 (Performance measures), then §3.2 through §3.4 and Table 4,
then the Discussion. Tables 1 and 2 are where the assignment actually lives — Table 2 is the record
of what was delivered as opposed to what was planned, and a learner who reads the abstract's "up to
4200 ppm" without reading Table 2's measured column will answer the `levels` rubric point wrongly.

The statistical reporting is ANOVA F-statistics with estimated marginal means and confidence
intervals rather than standardised effect sizes, which is worth flagging to a learner who has been
taught to look for Cohen's *d*. The relevant comparison is between the confidence intervals in
Table 4 and the ordering of the exposure groups, not a single number.

Skip nothing in §4, but do not stall in the VOC discussion — the VOC arm of this study did not work,
the authors say so, and for ARES's purposes the CO2 arm is the one that carries the assignment.

## Synthesis

### Why this paper, and why it is the awkward one

The obvious choice for M4 was a paper that supports the module. There are several, Satish being the
best known. Assigning one of them would have produced a lit review in which a learner reads a study
that agrees with the reading they just finished, writes down that it agrees, and learns nothing they
did not already have.

This paper disagrees with the reading, and that is the point.

`C15`'s dose–response table lists cognitive decrements in the 1,000–2,500 ppm tier. The largest
controlled test ever run of exactly that claim found nothing systematic up to 4,200 ppm in 398
people. A member who can hold both of those facts, state precisely what each one is worth, and still
explain why the project is worth doing is a member who can be trusted to describe ARES to a reviewer.
A member who cannot will either overclaim the physiology or conclude the whole project is pointless,
and both of those failures happen in this club regularly.

The specific thing to watch for while grading is the collapse in either direction. "Satish is small
and old, so Herbig settles it" and "Herbig is industry-funded, so it can be discounted" are the same
mistake wearing different clothes. Neither is an argument; both are ways of avoiding one.

### The move the rubric is really testing

Underneath the five points there is one skill, and it is the same one M3 installs against a modelling
paper: **work out what the design can establish, then check whether the conclusion stays inside it.**

Herbig et al. designed a study that can establish something about bulk cabin concentration, in
healthy screened adults, over about three hours, at partial pressures below NASA's operational limit.
Within that box the evidence is good and the null is meaningful. The conclusion they draw stays
inside the box; the headline someone will write from it will not.

ARES's premise lives entirely outside that box. It is about a localized concentration in front of a
face, in a person held stationary for hours, at partial pressures nobody has measured. Nothing in
this paper reaches it. That is not a rescue of the ARES premise from an inconvenient result — it is
the observation that the two are not about the same quantity, which is the observation the whole
course is built on.

A learner who gets there has understood M4. A learner who gets there and *also* notices that this
weakens the cognitive-decrement framing in the ARES deck, and that the deck should probably be
rewritten, has understood something the team has not yet acted on.

### On the partial-pressure conversion

The `levels` rubric point offers full credit for converting the exposures to mmHg, and that is
deliberate rather than decorative. It is the first place in the course where the units convention
from `GLOSSARY.md` §5 does real work instead of being a house-style rule.

The arithmetic is three lines. 940 hPa is 705 mmHg; 755 hPa is 566 mmHg. The highest measured group
average in the study, about 3,748 ppm at 940 hPa, is 3,748 × 10⁻⁶ × 705 ≈ 2.64 mmHg. NASA's
operational limit is 4 mmHg. So the study's most extreme condition sits about a third below the
limit the ISS is run against, and the arms at the higher ppm figures but lower cabin pressure are
lower still.

Nobody grading this should require that conversion. A learner who does it unprompted has stopped
treating ppm and mmHg as two spellings of the same thing, which is the single most useful habit M4
can leave behind.

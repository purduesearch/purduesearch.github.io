# C10 — The officer's handbook

> CONTENT section · Officer & Admin Tools · M1 · ~4 min read
> Follows V09 and the admin tour. Those show you where the controls are. This is the part you can't
> learn by being shown a screen: the standards, the cadence, and the four decisions that are actually
> yours.
> **This is the handover document.** If you're reading it because you just inherited the officer
> account and nobody explained anything, you're in the right place.

---

Almost nothing on the admin surface is difficult to operate. All of it is easy to get wrong, because
every control there is **club-wide, real, and has no sandbox.** A training project can hold a
practice task; it cannot hold a practice reward config.

So this page is about judgement, in the order you'll need it.

## 1 · The pending reward queue

Task completions and any time log over two hours in one entry wait here before paying out.

### The standard

You are **not** auditing anyone. You're checking that four hours of work looks like four hours of
work. Most entries are ten seconds and yes.

| What you see | What it usually is | Do |
|---|---|---|
| Plausible hours on a real task | Exactly what it looks like | Approve |
| 8 h in one entry on a small task | A whole week logged at once, or the wrong task | Reject, with a reason |
| Time on a task nothing else shows movement on | Reading, debugging, research — all real work | Approve |
| A burst of tiny tasks created and closed together | Sometimes real. Sometimes not | Ask, don't guess |

That last row is the only one that ever needs a conversation, and the conversation is genuinely
"hey, walk me through this week" — not an accusation. On a student team the answer is nearly always
mundane.

### Reject with a reason. Always.

> **A silent rejection teaches someone to stop logging time**, and then the club loses the data
> permanently. This is the single most damaging thing an officer can do with this queue, and it takes
> less effort than doing it right.

Wording that works, because it assumes the mistake rather than the motive:

- *"Looks like this went on the wrong task — same eight hours on the harness one? Re-log it there and
  I'll approve it."*
- *"Think this is a whole week in one entry. Split it per day and it'll go straight through."*
- *"Can you add a note on what this covered? Happy to approve, just want the record to make sense
  later."*

### The cadence

**Clear it at least twice a week.** Nothing is lost by waiting — but the feedback loop is, and the
loop is the entire product.

> A member who finishes something in week one and sees the recognition in week four has learned that
> the system doesn't notice. That's the exact failure the reward system exists to prevent, and an
> unattended queue causes it while looking like diligence.

If your queue is chronically slow, that's a staffing problem to say out loud, not a personal failing
to absorb quietly.

## 2 · Reward configuration

You can change what every event type is worth. The controls are trivial; the decision is not.

**The protocol, in order:**

1. **Say it first.** Announce the change and the reason, in the channel where members will see it.
2. **Wait a few days.** Long enough that nobody finds out by noticing their number moved.
3. **Then change it.**
4. **Don't retro-adjust.** Past grants stay as granted. Rewriting history is worse than any number.

> Members plan around these numbers. Quietly halving what logging time is worth reads as a punishment
> even when it was pure bookkeeping — and once people believe the numbers move without warning, they
> stop treating any of it as real.

Change these rarely. If you're editing reward config more than once a semester, the problem you're
solving is probably not a reward-config problem.

## 3 · Roles, and the sentence to remember

> **Rank is recognition. Role is access.**

What a member is permitted to do is governed by their **role** and by which **projects** they belong
to. Rank — the XP ladder — grants nothing at all. A Nestling officer can do everything a Celestial
officer can, and a Celestial with no officer role can do nothing an ordinary member can't.

Officers who forget this start treating XP as a permission system, and members immediately start
optimising for XP instead of for work. Say the sentence out loud when someone asks.

Two practical notes on member admin:

- **Grant the narrowest role that does the job**, and grant it promptly. Access withheld out of
  caution is just as much a blocker as any other.
- **Link GitHub logins.** It's a two-minute job that connects commits and pull requests to people,
  and it silently improves every report the club produces afterwards.

## 4 · Seeing everything

Officers can open every project, including ones they're not a member of.

> **Use that to unstick things, not to browse.** It's a maintenance capability, and the fastest way
> to make members stop writing honestly in their own boards is for them to feel watched in them.

The same restraint applies to the audit trail and the time-log history. They exist so the club can
answer questions about *work*. They're not a management dashboard for people.

## What isn't your job

Officers reliably burn out on things the system already does.

| Don't | Because |
|---|---|
| Chase overdue tasks manually | Reminders and escalations already fire on schedule |
| Nag about follow-ups | Overdue CRM follow-ups DM their owner on weekday mornings |
| Compile a weekly status summary | The Monday digest already posts one |
| Watch milestone health daily | It's recalculated every morning and surfaces itself |
| Approve every change request personally | Sub-team leads should review their own area's CRs |

Your scarce resource is attention. Spend it on the queue, on the four judgement calls above, and on
the conversations that no automation can have.

## Take away exactly two things

**Clear the queue often. Change the numbers rarely, and in public.**

Everything else on the admin surface is configuration you'll touch once a year. Those two habits are
what decide whether the club's record of its own work is worth anything.

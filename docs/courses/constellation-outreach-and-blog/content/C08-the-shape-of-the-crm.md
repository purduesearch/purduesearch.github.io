# C08 — The shape of the CRM

> CONTENT section · Outreach & the Blog · M1 · ~4 min read
> Reference companion to V07. The video explains why the club keeps contacts centrally; this is the
> page that defines the fields, the stages, and the two habits that decide whether a CRM is useful or
> just a tidier place to lose things.

---

Every club's outreach dies the same way. One person builds three good sponsor relationships, keeps
them in their head and a spreadsheet on their own Drive, and then graduates. The next committee
starts from a cold email list.

The CRM exists to stop that, and it only works if the record is written down while it's boring.

## A contact record, field by field

| Field | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **Name, org, role, email, phone** | Who they are. The bare minimum for a hand-off |
| **Type** | Sponsor · Press · Partner · Prospect · Alumni — what kind of relationship this is |
| **Stage** | How warm they are, right now. See below |
| **Owner** | The one member answerable for this relationship |
| **Tags** | Whatever you need to slice on later — "aerospace", "Indy", "gave last year" |
| **Notes** | Standing context. Not a log — the log is the timeline |
| **Last contacted** | Set automatically when an interaction is logged |
| **Next follow-up** | A date. The single most important field on the record |
| **Campaign** | Which push this contact is currently part of, if any |

### The five stages

| Stage | Means |
|---|---|
| **Cold** | We know they exist. Nobody has spoken to them |
| **Contacted** | We reached out. No reply yet |
| **Engaged** | They replied. There's a conversation |
| **Active** | A live relationship — sponsoring, hosting, collaborating now |
| **Dormant** | Was active, has gone quiet. Not dead, just parked |

Stage is a column on the pipeline board, and moving a card is how you update it.

> **Cold and Dormant are not the same thing, and the difference is worth money.** Cold is a stranger.
> Dormant is somebody who has already said yes to this club once. When next year's committee is
> deciding who to email first, that distinction is the whole answer — and it only exists if somebody
> moved the card instead of quietly stopping.

## The timeline is the actual product

The **Info** tab is who somebody is. The **Timeline** is what has happened — every interaction, typed
as an email, meeting, call, note, or event attended, with who from the club was involved and when.

That log is the part that survives you. Three rules:

- **Log what was promised, not just that you spoke.** "Met Dana at the career fair" is worth little.
  "Met Dana at the career fair — she asked for a one-page capability summary before their Q3
  budgeting in August" is worth the entire record.
- **Log it the same day.** The version you write a week later is shorter and wrong in the specific
  detail that mattered.
- **Log the bad ones too.** "Called, not interested this year, try again after the rebrand" saves the
  next person a wasted afternoon and an awkward second cold email.

## Follow-ups: a date and an owner, or it isn't real

> **"We should reach out sometime" is not a follow-up.** Nothing reminds anyone, nobody is
> answerable, and it will be true and unactioned for the rest of the year.

A follow-up needs exactly two things: a **date** and a **named owner**. With both, Constellation
carries it for you — overdue follow-ups arrive as a Slack DM on weekday mornings, so the CRM comes to
you rather than waiting to be reopened.

That nudge is the entire difference between a CRM and a list of good intentions. It's also why
setting a slightly wrong date beats setting none: a date you have to push back is a decision, and a
blank field is an absence nobody ever notices.

Set the follow-up **before you save the contact**, while the reason for it is still in your head.

## Campaigns

A campaign groups the contacts you're working through together, with a name, an owner, dates, and a
goal — recruitment, sponsorship, an event, or general awareness.

The value isn't the grouping. It's **coverage**: at a glance, who's been contacted, who replied, and
who has been sitting untouched for three weeks.

> That last column is the one that matters. Outreach almost never fails because a message was badly
> written; it fails because forty contacts got one email each and nobody went back.

Two practical notes:

- **Name it after the ask, not the season.** "Fall 2026 Push" tells a future member nothing.
  "Machine shop sponsorship — spring build" tells them exactly what was being asked and of whom.
- **A campaign is a push, not a filing cabinet.** Contacts belong to the club permanently; they join
  a campaign for as long as that push runs.

## The composer, and the line not to cross

Constellation can draft outreach messages, and it can draft them in one of the club's saved **brand
voices** so a sponsor email and an Instagram caption don't read like the same person wrote them.

It is a drafting aid. Always:

1. **Read the whole thing.** Out loud, if it's going to a sponsor.
2. **Fix the specifics.** Generated copy is confidently vague about numbers, dates, and what the club
   actually built. Those are the parts a sponsor reads.
3. **Cut a third of it.** Drafts run long.

> A sponsor can tell when nobody looked at what they were sent, and the cost of that impression is
> far higher than the ten minutes it saved. The club's name is on it, not the tool's.

**Nothing in the walkthrough after this page sends a message.** Composing is practised on drafts
only — an outbound send is the one action in this whole curriculum that could reach a real person by
accident.

## Two habits, and you're done

**Log the interaction the day it happens. Set a next follow-up with a date and an owner.**

Everything else — the stages, the campaigns, the coverage view, the morning DM — is machinery built
on top of those two habits. Without them it's an empty database with good intentions; with them it's
the thing that makes a sponsor relationship belong to the club instead of to whoever happened to
answer the email.

# C09 — From draft to public

> CONTENT section · Outreach & the Blog · M2 · ~4 min read
> Reference companion to V08. Read this before the walkthrough — its step 8 spotlights the Publish
> button and tells you **not** to press it, and that instruction makes a lot more sense once you know
> what pressing it does.

---

The blog is the club's public voice. It's the page a prospective sponsor lands on after your email,
the thing a first-year reads before deciding which sub-team to join, and — because it's indexed — the
first result for the club's name for years afterwards.

Which is why the editor is deliberately relaxed about everything except the last step.

## The four states of a post

| State | Who can see it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Draft** | Members with the editor | The default. Free. Make as many as you like |
| **Scheduled** | Members | Publishes itself at the time you set — the scheduler checks every few minutes |
| **Published** | **Anyone on the internet** | Live on the public site under the club's name |
| **Archived** | Members | Pulled from the public site, kept in the record |

Drafts are cheap. Abandoned drafts cost nothing and nobody minds them. There is no reason to write a
post anywhere other than in here.

## Writing together

The editor is genuinely collaborative — the same kind of thing as a shared document. If another
member opens the post while you're in it, you'll see their cursor and their changes appear as they
type, and yours appear for them.

So the answer to "someone else is editing, what do I do" is: **keep writing.** There's no version to
send anyone, nothing to merge afterwards, and nothing to check out. Any instinct you brought from the
Vault does not apply here — a CAD file has one true state, a paragraph does not.

It autosaves about a second and a half after you stop typing. **Save draft** exists for when you want
to be sure; you rarely need it. Every save also builds a revision history, so a paragraph somebody
deleted on Tuesday is recoverable.

## Use the built-in styles

Headings, callouts, quotes, section blocks, image blocks — use those rather than formatting text by
hand.

> The public site does **not** display what you see in the editor. It renders stored structure
> through its own renderer, which knows how to lay out a heading and a callout and nothing about
> hand-spaced bold text. Manual formatting survives the editor and breaks on the live page, usually
> on mobile, usually after publish.

Same rule for images: **drop the 6 MB photo straight in.** Uploads are resized, compressed, and
converted for you. Preparing images by hand takes your evening and produces a worse result than the
pipeline.

## The fields that aren't the body

Easy to skip, and each one has a job:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Slug** | The permanent URL. Changing it after publish breaks every existing link — get it right first |
| **Excerpt** | The card text on the blog index. Written badly, it's the first sentence truncated mid-word |
| **Cover image** | The card, and usually the social preview |
| **Meta description / OG fields** | What appears when the link is pasted into Slack, LinkedIn, or a text |
| **Authors** | Credit. Multiple members can be listed, and publishing earns the author XP |
| **Tags / categories** | How anyone finds this post in two years |

The social preview fields matter more than they look. A sponsor pasting your post into their internal
chat and getting a bare URL with no title is a small, avoidable loss.

## The AI review panel

Open the assistant and it reads the draft and suggests things. Nothing reads your draft in the
background — it runs when you ask it to.

Treat it as **a second reader with opinions**, some of which are good:

- **Good at:** flabby sentences, structural repetition, headline options, a plain-English pass over
  a paragraph that got too technical.
- **Bad at:** knowing which details carry meaning for this club. It will confidently propose cutting
  the specific number, the member's name, or the awkward true detail — which are often the only
  reasons the post is worth reading.

Accept and reject on the merits, individually. Applying every suggestion produces something fluent,
correct, and completely generic.

## Publishing

> **Publishing is the one irreversible action in this course.**

It makes the post readable by anyone, under the club's name, on the public internet. Within minutes
it may be cached, indexed, and pasted somewhere you can't reach. Un-publishing pulls the page down;
it does not un-send it.

It is **not** officer-gated. Nobody is going to catch a mistake for you. That's a deliberate choice —
gating publishing would mean posts wait a week for an officer who's in exams — but it means the
check is yours.

Before you press it:

- [ ] Read the whole post once more, top to bottom, out loud
- [ ] Every name spelled right; every number checked against something real
- [ ] Nobody identifiable in a photo who wouldn't want to be
- [ ] No sponsor named as a sponsor before that's been agreed in writing
- [ ] Slug is right — it's permanent
- [ ] Excerpt and social preview read well as a card
- [ ] Someone else has read it

That last one is not optional in spirit, even though the software allows it. **Get a second pair of
eyes.** It's the cheapest quality control the club has and the one it most reliably skips.

If the post is time-sensitive — a competition result, an event announcement — **schedule it** instead
of staying up. Scheduling is a real publish with a delay, so the checklist above applies at the
moment you schedule, not at the moment it goes out.

## The short version

Drafts are free, collaboration is automatic, formatting should come from the toolbar, images need no
preparation, and the AI is a colleague rather than an editor.

Then, once — deliberately, having read it aloud and had someone else read it too — you publish.
